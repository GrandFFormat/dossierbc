Tous les projets

✋ What is “challenging”?

One click on “Ask for an explanation” = a citizen request for the bill's sponsor to explain it in plain language, and for us to keep an eye on it together.

An account (email) is required — one request per person, no anonymous requests.

In British Columbia, these requests lead to nothing official: the Assembly has no online petitions, unlike Quebec or the House of Commons. For now, it is a simple way to see which laws trouble people. Some day, an elected member may find a use for it.

On the “who voted what” detail — the Legislative Assembly keeps no register of votes: each MLA's name is only written in its daily Journals, during a recorded division. The by-member breakdown above comes from those Journals, not an estimate. A vote where even one name can't be matched to the list of MLAs is not published. How to read a vote

Recorded division

Each MLA stands and their choice is recorded in the Journals of the Legislative Assembly. It's the only kind of vote that can be attributed to a person.

Passed ≠ law

A winning vote moves the bill one stage forward. It only becomes law after assent by the Lieutenant-Governor.

Party line

Most votes follow the party's instruction. Free votes are rare — and that's where the nominal detail gets interesting.

Past legislatures What came before.

A legislature ends and everything it had begun dies with it. Nothing is deleted here: the bills, the recorded votes and the people of each finished Parliament keep their page, exactly as they were on the last day.

    The lobbyist registry

    Who's paid to influence public decisions — and how to look it up

    Anyone paid to communicate with public office holders (ministers, MLAs, senior officials…) in order to influence a decision must register in British Columbia's Lobbyists Registry, kept by the Office of the Registrar of Lobbyists.

    Why this site does not reproduce the registry: we have not asked the Registrar for permission, and we do not copy a public register without it. The search tool below is the official one, and it stays the reference.

    Parliamentary glossary

    To understand the rest of the site without a political science degree
    Bill
    Process
    The text a member or the government submits to the Assembly to become a law. It only becomes a real law once passed and then assented to.
    Second reading
    Process
    The first big step: the Assembly debates the general idea of the bill and votes on whether it accepts it, before studying the details clause by clause.
    Committee stage
    Process
    The step where the bill is gone over clause by clause, most often in Committee of the Whole (the entire Assembly sitting as a committee), sometimes by a standing committee. Amendments can be proposed there.
    Third reading
    Process
    The Assembly's final vote on the bill as a whole, once the committee stage is over.
    Royal Assent
    Process
    The very last step: the Lieutenant-Governor gives official assent, and the bill formally becomes a law of British Columbia.
    Committee
    Process
    A smaller group of MLAs who study a subject or a bill in depth, may hear from experts and citizens, then report back to the full Assembly.
    Brief (to a committee)
    Process
    A document that an organization, a business or a citizen submits to an Assembly committee to say what they think of a bill or a subject under study: what they support, what should change, what worries them. Some are invited to present it in person to the MLAs.
    Brief or lobbying?
    Process
    Both try to influence a decision, but not in the same way. A brief is a public position, within a committee's official process: you know who is speaking and on what subject. Lobbying happens in private: meetings, calls or emails with a minister, a minister's office or public servants. The law only requires that it be entered in the province's lobbyists' registry, which says who is lobbying, for whom and on what subject, never what was said.
    Recorded vote
    Votes
    A vote where each MLA's name and choice (for or against) are written in the Assembly's Journals — as opposed to a voice vote, where only the result is noted. In British Columbia, most bills pass by voice vote.
    Journal of the Assembly
    Votes
    The official, summarized record of each sitting: decisions, votes, documents tabled. It is there, and only there, that names are written down during a recorded division.
    Debates journal (Journal des débats)
    Votes
    The complete, word-for-word transcript of everything said in the House — unlike the Journal of the Assembly, which only summarizes the decisions.
    House leader
    People & roles
    The MLA responsible for organizing their group's proceedings at the Assembly — a bit like the conductor of the calendar and debates for their party.
    Whip
    People & roles
    The MLA in charge of making sure members of their parliamentary group are present for votes and follow the party line.
    Minister Delegate / Minister Responsible for
    People & roles
    A minister who isn't the main head of a department, but who officially holds responsibility for a specific file within or alongside it.
    Attendance (%)
    Citizen tools
    The figure shown under each MLA: out of the recorded divisions of the House, the share in which this person appears as for or against — meaning they were present to vote, whichever way they voted. Divisions held in committee (Committee of the Whole, Committee of Supply, in Section A, B or C) are left out: only about a dozen members sit there, and counting them would make everyone look absent. The count starts when the legislature opens. The Assembly publishes no official attendance; this is an indicator of presence, not an official measure, and a missed vote can be an official mission, an illness or a scheduling conflict — the register does not give the reason.

    Where this data comes from, and how to go further

    Public sources used here

    Bills — leg.bc.ca, “Status of Legislation” for each session of the legislature, in French and English: stages, dates of each reading.
    Bill details (type, sponsor, official text) — leg.bc.ca, each bill's page in both languages.
    Bill summaries — generated by artificial intelligence (Claude API) from the official text as introduced, one pass per language; each summary is labelled as such on the site.
    Recorded divisions — the Assembly's daily Journals (PDF, leg.bc.ca), the only place where names are recorded.
    Executive Council — leg.bc.ca, Executive Council page, in French and English.
    MLAs — leg.bc.ca, official list of sitting members. Their email addresses are not shown: leg.bc.ca masks them.
    Attendance (%) — calculated from the recorded divisions above (see the definition in the glossary); no outside source.

    DossierBC's sourcing rules The rules, in black and white.

    The site only publishes what an institution or a party has itself published, as is, with a link to the original. And it says clearly what is missing.

    1. 1No invented data

      This is rule number one. If a piece of information is missing, the field stays empty or the site says so. We do not guess and we do not fill in.

    2. 2Primary sources only

      We quote the institution itself, never someone talking about it: British Columbia's Legislative Assembly (leg.bc.ca) for bills, their stages, the recorded divisions written in its Journals, the MLAs and the Executive Council.

    3. 3No news media

      Nothing is taken from the press. News outlets have withdrawn their content from use by artificial intelligence, and the site does not read them. What was said at a press conference but never recorded by the Assembly therefore does not appear here. One exception, named on the page where it is used: the October 24, 2026 election result is read from the Wikipedia article on that election, because Elections BC closes its site to automated readers.

    4. 4We respect a website's refusal

      If a website asks automated tools not to read it, we do not read it, even when it would be technically possible. That is the case for elections.bc.ca and bclaws.gov.bc.ca: we link to them and read nothing from them. The program that reads official websites identifies itself honestly, and waits the pace a site asks for when it sets one. We never work around a CAPTCHA or an anti-bot protection. The MLA email addresses shown here are the legislative addresses the Assembly publishes itself, so that people can write to their members — and nothing else: no personal address, no constituency-office address. When access is blocked, we write to the organization to ask for permission.

    5. 5A recorded division is a recorded division

      Names are copied from the Assembly's Votes and Proceedings, the only place they are recorded — never inferred from party membership. Most bills pass on a voice vote: the absence of a recorded division does not mean “unanimous”, and the site never writes that it does.

    6. 6Attendance counts House divisions only

      Committees — Committee of the Whole, Committee of Supply — sit in Section A, B or C with about a dozen members. Their divisions are not a vote of the House: counting them would make almost everyone look absent. The published rate therefore keeps only divisions of the House, and the glossary says how it is calculated.

    7. 7No verdict

      The site never says a bill is good or bad, nor that a vote was the right one. It places the facts side by side, each with its source. You are the judge.

    8. 8The official text always prevails

      The full text is one click away from every bill. Plain-language summaries are not written yet: each page says so, rather than showing something written without having read it. If anything differs, the original document is the one that counts. The site is independent and has no official status.

    9. 9We explain what is missing

      When a source is absent or incomplete, the page says so and says why. An absence must never be read as silence from a member or a party.

    These rules apply everywhere on the site. Follow what changes in the site updates. All the content on the site is free.

    If we want people to understand politics, it has to be brought down to their level.

    When I first tried to take an interest in it, on top of not knowing the jargon, bills came as two lines… and fifty PDFs. Most people would simply walk away.

    So I had an idea: use artificial intelligence first to read through all of it, then to put it in plain words. That's how DossierQuébec was born — and now DossierBC.
