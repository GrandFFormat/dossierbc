Who's involved around the Assembly

The public roles we hadn't covered yet — real names, real current functions
Not yet covered for British Columbia. This section lists the officers around an assembly — the Speaker, the Clerk, the Auditor General, the Ombudsperson, the Information and Privacy Commissioner. On this site it is empty on purpose: the Legislative Assembly publishes these appointments on its own pages, and we have not yet read them with a scraper. The lobbyist registry

Who's paid to influence public decisions — and how to look it up

Anyone paid to communicate with public office holders (ministers, MLAs, senior officials…) in order to influence a decision must register in British Columbia's Lobbyists Registry, kept by the Office of the Registrar of Lobbyists.

Why this site does not reproduce the registry: we have not asked the Registrar for permission, and we do not copy a public register without it. The search tool below is the official one, and it stays the reference.

Parliamentary glossary

To understand the rest of the site without a political science degree
Bill
Process
The text a member or the government submits to the Assembly to become a law. It only becomes a real law once passed and then assented to.
Second reading
Process
The first big step: the Assembly debates the general idea of the bill and votes on whether it accepts it, before studying the details clause by clause.
Committee stage
Process
The step where the bill is gone over clause by clause, most often in Committee of the Whole (the entire Assembly sitting as a committee), sometimes by a standing committee. Amendments can be proposed there.
Third reading
Process
The Assembly's final vote on the bill as a whole, once the committee stage is over.
Royal Assent
Process
The very last step: the Lieutenant-Governor gives official assent, and the bill formally becomes a law of British Columbia.
Committee
Process
A smaller group of MLAs who study a subject or a bill in depth, may hear from experts and citizens, then report back to the full Assembly.
Brief (to a committee)
Process
A document that an organization, a business or a citizen submits to an Assembly committee to say what they think of a bill or a subject under study: what they support, what should change, what worries them. Some are invited to present it in person to the MLAs.
Brief or lobbying?
Process
Both try to influence a decision, but not in the same way. A brief is a public position, within a committee's official process: you know who is speaking and on what subject. Lobbying happens in private: meetings, calls or emails with a minister, a minister's office or public servants. The law only requires that it be entered in the province's lobbyists' registry, which says who is lobbying, for whom and on what subject, never what was said.
Recorded vote
Votes
A vote where each MLA's name and choice (for or against) are written in the Assembly's Journals — as opposed to a voice vote, where only the result is noted. In British Columbia, most bills pass by voice vote.
Journal of the Assembly
Votes
The official, summarized record of each sitting: decisions, votes, documents tabled. It is there, and only there, that names are written down during a recorded division.
Debates journal (Journal des débats)
Votes
The complete, word-for-word transcript of everything said in the House — unlike the Journal of the Assembly, which only summarizes the decisions.
House leader
People & roles
The MLA responsible for organizing their group's proceedings at the Assembly — a bit like the conductor of the calendar and debates for their party.
Whip
People & roles
The MLA in charge of making sure members of their parliamentary group are present for votes and follow the party line.
Minister Delegate / Minister Responsible for
People & roles
A minister who isn't the main head of a department, but who officially holds responsibility for a specific file within or alongside it.
Attendance (%)
Citizen tools
The figure shown under each MLA: out of the recorded divisions of the House, the share in which this person appears as for or against — meaning they were present to vote, whichever way they voted. Divisions held in committee (Committee of the Whole, Committee of Supply, in Section A, B or C) are left out: only about a dozen members sit there, and counting them would make everyone look absent. The count starts when the legislature opens. The Assembly publishes no official attendance; this is an indicator of presence, not an official measure, and a missed vote can be an official mission, an illness or a scheduling conflict — the register does not give the reason.

Where this data comes from, and how to go further

Public sources used here

Bills — leg.bc.ca, “Status of Legislation” for each session of the legislature, in French and English: stages, dates of each reading.
Bill details (type, sponsor, official text) — leg.bc.ca, each bill's page in both languages.
Bill summaries — generated by artificial intelligence (Claude API) from the official text as introduced, one pass per language; each summary is labelled as such on the site.
Recorded divisions — the Assembly's daily Journals (PDF, leg.bc.ca), the only place where names are recorded.
Executive Council — leg.bc.ca, Executive Council page, in French and English.
MLAs — leg.bc.ca, official list of sitting members. Their email addresses are not shown: leg.bc.ca masks them.
Attendance (%) — calculated from the recorded divisions above (see the definition in the glossary); no outside source.
